Most analytics platforms are fast at loading and slow at *thinking*. You wait
for the dashboard, then every filter you touch makes you wait again.

This is a million taxi trips. **Drag a box on either map.** The other charts
re-aggregate before your hand stops moving — no loading state, no spinner, no
backend round trip per frame.

<NycTaxiRides />

Every frame of that drag re-aggregated a million rows into three charts in
**28–77 ms**. The architecture underneath is three pieces you already trust,
arranged in an order most teams haven't tried: **Parquet for storage, DuckDB
for the engine, Mosaic as the coordinator.**

This piece is about that arrangement — what it costs, where it stops working,
and why a platform team can build it in a week instead of buying it.

## The problem: the interaction budget

Take the obvious approach. The brush moves, every chart re-runs its query, each
query scans the table. Priced honestly on first load, before any index exists:

| Chart | Time | Rows returned |
|---|---|---|
| Pickups map | 58 ms | 62,114 |
| Dropoffs map | 105 ms | 78,319 |
| Hour histogram | 134 ms | 24 |

**About 300 ms for one frame.** Your hand generates pointer events every
8–16 ms. You fall behind on frame one and never catch up.

Worse, it's **O(N)**. Ten million rows means ten times the wait, forever. Every
"make the dashboard faster" project that ends in more indexes, more caching
layers, or a bigger warehouse cluster is fighting this exact curve — and paying
per query to lose.

The fix is not a faster scan. It is **not scanning during the interaction at
all.**

## Three pieces

### Parquet: the storage format, not just an export target

Parquet is columnar, compressed, and carries statistics per row group. Two
properties matter here:

- **Projection pushdown** — a query touching 4 of 30 columns reads 4 columns
  off disk. Row-oriented storage reads all 30.
- **Predicate pushdown** — row-group min/max lets the engine skip entire blocks
  without decompressing them.

A million trips with a dozen columns is **46 MB** as Parquet. The same data as
CSV is several hundred. That difference is the whole reason this page loads.

### DuckDB: an engine that reads Parquet natively

DuckDB queries Parquet directly — no load step, no ingest job, no copy into a
proprietary format:

```sql
SELECT count(*), avg(fare)
FROM 'trips.parquet'
WHERE hour BETWEEN 17 AND 19
```

It's vectorized and columnar end to end, so aggregating a million rows is
milliseconds on one core. It runs as a library inside your server process, in a
notebook, or — as on this page — compiled to WebAssembly inside the browser tab.

Same SQL, same engine, three deployment targets. Hold that thought.

### Mosaic: the coordinator

This is the piece most data teams haven't met, and it's the one doing the
interesting work.

[Mosaic](https://idl.uw.edu/mosaic/) sits between your views and DuckDB. Every
chart registers as a *client*. Every filter is a *selection*. Nothing queries
the database directly — the **coordinator** does, on everyone's behalf.

That indirection buys four things no individual chart could arrange for itself:

- It resolves one selection into **one predicate**, shared by every view.
- It **deduplicates and queues** concurrent queries.
- It **caches** identical statements.
- And — the reason we're here — it decides when to **build a cube.**

## Phase 1: the cube is built on hover

**When your pointer enters a chart — before you press anything — Mosaic
materializes index tables.** One per view that can use one. Here that's three
`CREATE TABLE` statements, 62 / 92 / 146 ms.

You never feel it, because you are still moving your hand toward the thing you
intend to click.

Here is what one of those tables actually is:

```sql
CREATE TABLE mosaic.preagg_5e8541db AS
SELECT floor(time)     AS x1,               -- the histogram's own buckets
       floor(time) + 1 AS x2,
       count(*)        AS pre_e87fc4ab,     -- the partial answer
       floor(0.01305 * (px - 970000))::INT AS active0,  -- brush position, in pixels
       floor(0.01306 * (py - 188000))::INT AS active1
FROM trips
GROUP BY x1, x2, active0, active1
```

Read it as a grid: **the chart's own buckets crossed with every position the
brush could possibly take**, with the answer already computed for each cell.

If you've built OLAP cubes, this will feel familiar — because it is one. The
difference is that nobody designed it. Mosaic derived the cube's shape from the
chart's encoding and the interactor's geometry, then built it during a gesture
the user doesn't perceive as waiting.

Dragging then becomes **arithmetic, not aggregation** — sum the cells whose
brush position falls inside the current box:

```sql
SELECT x1, x2, sum(pre_e87fc4ab) AS y
FROM mosaic.preagg_5e8541db
WHERE active0 BETWEEN 214 AND 287
  AND active1 BETWEEN 122 AND 190
GROUP BY x1, x2
```

No coordinates recomputed. No million rows scanned. A range-sum over a table
built for this exact gesture.

## Phase 2: three queries per frame

The figure has **four views**: the pickups map, the dropoffs map, the hour
histogram, and the KPI tiles. While you drag the pickups map, **three queries
run per frame:**

| View | Reads | Time | Returns |
|---|---|---|---|
| KPI tiles | cube | 36 ms | 1 row · **32 B** |
| Dropoffs map | cube | 14 ms | 59,740 rows · **891 KB** |
| Hour histogram | cube | 36 ms | 24 rows · **576 B** |

Three things here surprise people, and each is a design lesson.

**The map you're dragging isn't in the list.** A view is filtered by every
selection *except its own* — otherwise your brush would erase the context you
are selecting against. Dragging the pickups map costs nothing on the pickups
map.

**Four tiles is one query, not four.** The unit of work is the *component*, not
the number on screen:

```sql
SELECT count(*) AS trips, avg(distance) AS distance,
       avg(fare) AS fare,  avg(riders)   AS riders
FROM trips
```

The three charts stay three queries, though, because they ask genuinely
different questions — different `GROUP BY`, different measures, 24 buckets vs
59,740 pixels vs 1 row. No single result set answers all three without shipping
raw rows to the client and aggregating there, which is the design this exists
to avoid. **Three queries is correct.** What changed is which table they read.

**Cost barely tracks result size.** The histogram returns 24 rows and costs more
than the map returning 59,740. What matters is the size of the cube, not the
size of the answer.

## Only the filtered data crosses the wire

Look at the bytes column again: **32 B, 891 KB, 576 B.**

That is the enterprise headline. The engine sits next to the data — 46 MB of
Parquet — and what leaves it is the *aggregate*, sized by the chart's
resolution, not the table's row count. The million rows never move.

This inverts how most BI tools behave, where the client pulls a result set and
does the last mile of work itself, and payload grows with your data.

Notice too that the figure narrates itself: a status line reporting whether data
came from cache or network, per-query timings, the SQL behind every box.
**Hint text is not decoration.** When a number moves and the user can see why,
they trust it; when it moves silently, they open a ticket. It's the cheapest
trust feature you will ever ship, and the first one cut from most internal
tools.

## Same architecture, two deployments

This page fetches Parquet from a CDN, caches it in the browser, and runs
DuckDB-WASM in the tab. Zero backend. That's one deployment of the pattern, and
it's the right one for a surprising amount of internal tooling.

The architecture doesn't change when the data outgrows a tab. Mosaic ships three
connectors:

| Connector | DuckDB runs | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| `wasmConnector` | in the browser tab | ≤ ~100 MB · internal tools, embedded analytics, static sites |
| `socketConnector` | a DuckDB server process | GB-scale · shared, governed, multi-tenant |
| `restConnector` | behind your own HTTP API | when you need auth, row-level security, audit |

**Your charts, selections and cubes don't know which one you picked.** Views
talk to the coordinator; the coordinator talks to a connector. Swapping
transports is a line of configuration, not a rewrite.

So a team can prototype entirely in-browser against a sampled Parquet extract,
then move the same code to a server-side DuckDB reading from object storage when
governance or volume demands it. The cube strategy, the three-queries-per-frame
shape, and the payload sizes are identical. Only the hop changes.

## The enterprise shape: snapshot, then serve

Most enterprise analytics is **not** asking questions of live transactional
state. It's asking questions of a period that has closed — yesterday, last week,
last quarter. That distinction is where this architecture earns its keep.

```
Postgres / Oracle / Snowflake        ← system of record, stays authoritative
        │
        │  scheduled snapshot  (nightly · weekly · quarterly)
        ▼
    Parquet on object storage        ← immutable, partitioned, cheap
        │
        ▼
  DuckDB + Mosaic coordinator        ← every interaction served from here
```

What that buys:

- **The OLTP database stops absorbing analytical load.** Every drag on a
  dashboard is a query your transactional system never sees.
- **Snapshots are immutable, so results are reproducible.** "The Q3 number
  changed since Tuesday" stops being a conversation.
- **Cost becomes a schedule, not a meter.** You pay for one extract on a cadence
  you chose, instead of per-query against a warehouse billing by the second
  while a user fiddles with a filter.
- **Partitioning is just file layout** — `year=/month=/region=` directories, and
  DuckDB prunes them without a metastore.

Match the cadence to the question. Finance reporting: quarterly. Ops review:
weekly. Sales pipeline: nightly. The interaction budget is the same 30 ms in
every case, because a snapshot's freshness has nothing to do with a cube's
speed.

## Why a team should build this

For small and mid-sized use cases, the honest comparison isn't DuckDB-and-Mosaic
versus a better dashboard. It's versus a platform licence, a semantic layer you
don't control, and a roadmap you can't influence.

What building it yourself gets you:

- **The visual grammar is yours.** Your charts, your interactions, your empty
  states, your copy — not the six widgets a vendor exposes.
- **Domain logic lives in SQL you own** — your definition of an active customer,
  your fiscal calendar, your exclusions. Not in someone's modelling UI.
- **It embeds where the work happens.** Inside your internal app, next to the
  record the user is actually looking at, not behind a separate login.
- **No per-seat cost**, so analysis goes in front of everyone who'd benefit
  rather than everyone who can justify a licence.
- **It's inspectable.** Every query is SQL you can read, log, test and defend in
  a review.

The reason this is now a week of work rather than a quarter is that the hard
parts — the engine, the format, the coordination — are solved and free. What's
left is the part only you can do: deciding what your users should be able to ask.

## When to reach for this, and when not to

Size it in **rows and columns**, not megabytes.

| Rows | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| Under ~100 K | Ship JSON, filter it in JavaScript. No engine, no cube, no point. |
| **100 K – 10 M** | **This architecture, in the browser.** |
| Over ~10 M | Same code, `socketConnector`, DuckDB on a server. |

Above 10 M it isn't the queries that break — it's the load. Nobody should pull
that into a tab before seeing anything.

Two rules worth keeping:

- **Rows are cheap, columns are not.** More rows cost load time and memory but
  *not* per-frame latency — the cube is sized by chart resolution, not row
  count. More projected columns cost you everywhere at once.
- **Only the columns your charts touch count.** Parquet reads what you select,
  so a 200-column fact table where the dashboard uses twelve behaves like a
  twelve-column table.

The trade underneath fits in one line: **pay one large cost up front, at a moment
the user isn't waiting, to make every subsequent question free.**

## What I'm building next

The snapshot cadence above is currently a human decision — someone chooses
nightly or weekly and writes a cron entry. That's the obvious place for an agent.

I'm exploring a planner that looks at a question and decides: **is this ad hoc,
or is it becoming a workload?**

A one-off exploration runs against the source and costs what it costs. But if
the same question is asked forty times a week, the system should recognise that
pattern and consider turning it into a scheduled extract — with its own Parquet
snapshot and potentially its own pre-aggregated representation.

The agent wouldn't execute that optimisation. It would **detect the pattern,
propose the configuration, estimate the cost/benefit, and justify the decision
against a defined budget** — leaving the commit to a human.

Cost-aware query planning, one level above the optimizer. That's the next post.
